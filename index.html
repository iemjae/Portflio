About

An engineer who fell for patent claims.

I follow curiosity. It started with my old Samsung smartphone: I took it apart and replaced its camera myself, and I’ve wanted to know what’s underneath things ever since.

That turned into a degree in Electronics & Communication Engineering at Panjab University, and into instrumentation and signal processing at labs like CSIO, DRDO and Panjab University. There I learned to build the thing, measure it, and let the measurement correct the story I’d told myself. In 2021, PGIMER invited me to speak on using Python to analyse and visualise experimental data.

In 2023 I joined Copperpod IP and found that a patent claim isn’t dense legal text. It’s a precise technical description of how an invention behaves, and reading one well takes the same mind that could have built it. Today I work as a senior analyst on standard-essential patents and 3GPP, where the engineering background isn’t a detour; it’s the toolkit.

How I think

What the work keeps teaching me.

  1. i.

    Filings arrive before products do.

    Patents are technical disclosure that precedes commercialisation by years. A single patent rarely says much; the pattern across a portfolio does.

    Read the article
  2. ii.

    IP analysis without engineering depth is confident guessing.

    You can only judge “a processor configured to…” if you know what a processor really does at the instruction level. Matching words isn’t analysis; it’s pattern matching.

    Read the article
  3. iii.

    Standards are negotiated, not discovered.

    Every 3GPP release is a consensus between hundreds of companies, and the negotiation leaves traces: in proposals, in working-group minutes, and eventually in patent claims.

    Read the article
  4. iv.

    Reality and documentation quietly disagree.

    Specs describe how a product should behave; firmware and hardware show how it does. A lot of good analysis lives in the space between the two.

The book

3GPP Through The Ages: A Guidebook

Behind every call, text and video stream sits 3GPP, the quiet powerhouse that standardises the wireless world.

The book traces wireless from 1G all the way to the promise of 6G, and explains the decisions behind LTE, 5G NR and the rest in language a curious person can actually follow: how each generation built on the last, what LTE and 5G NR actually changed, the architecture beneath your smartphone, and why standards quietly shape whole industries.

It’s written for students, tinkerers, and anyone curious about what powers the connected world, not just the specialists who already speak the language.

Alongside the IP work

On the bench.

Patents are the day job. Engineering is the habit that keeps my reading honest. I’m currently going deeper into embedded systems, and these are a few of the things I’ve built along the way.

Embedded systems

Now · STM32 · C · RTOS · RF / SDR

What I’m studying and building now: firmware on STM32, moving from bare-metal code to real-time operating systems, alongside RF and SDR spectrum experiments. I still take apart commercial IoT products to see how firmware and hardware implement what the spec promises.

TDLAS gas-sensing instrument

CSIO · RedPitaya · embedded firmware · Python · MATLAB

A laser absorption spectroscopy rig on a RedPitaya platform, with embedded firmware and a real-time Python pipeline. Acquiring the signal was easy; trusting it wasn’t. Custom baseline correction brought it to 90% measurement accuracy with 75% less noise, and to a working prototype.

SpectroPlotter

Raspberry Pi · RGB light sensing · Python GUI

A low-cost, portable spectroscope built from RGB light sensing and a Raspberry Pi, with a small GUI for offline spectral visualisation. Proof that useful instrumentation doesn’t need an expensive bench, just careful interfacing.

3-lead ECG monitor

Arduino · analog front-end · digital filtering · MATLAB

A real-time biosignal acquisition system with an analog front-end, digital filtering and MATLAB baseline correction. A lesson in how fragile a physiological signal really is, and how much processing hides behind a clean trace.
